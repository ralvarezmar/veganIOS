# Deliver the content people need

Ensure your documentation provides the information your audience needs but doesn't have yet. To do this, you need to understand who are the people who are going to look for and read your texts.

People in the same role often have skills, foundational knowledge, and questions in common, although this metric alone is not enough. Let's take a look at some of them:

- Who is your target audience?
- Why are you reading this documentation?
- What do your readers already know before they read your documentation?
- What should your readers know or be able to do after they've read your documentation?

## Determine the basic knowledge needed

List what topics someone needs to be aware of before they start reading your content, such as:

> "You will need to have prior knowledge in ***Javascript***, ***Typescript*** and the basics of ***HTML*** and ***CSS***".

This will help people understand if that text is right for them and, if they want to continue, what topics they would need to learn.

## Define the goals of your article

At the end of the reading, what knowledge and skills will be acquired by the audience? Bring this information in a very objective way. For example:

> "By the end, you'll be able to send and receive emails, check your inbox, and save favorite messages."

In this way, you ensure that the audience is very clear about what the gain is from reading your content.

## Abstract very specific concepts and vocabulary

Pay attention to the context. People on your team understand project jargon and abbreviations, but do people on other teams understand those same terms?

After a long time working with the same technology or project, it's easy to forget that beginners don't know what you already know. As your target audience grows, you should explain more details, or define what knowledge is prerequisites.
